Where we stand

What do these mean?

In the Paris Agreement, countries agreed to keep warming well below 2°C and to try to stop at 1.5°C. These limits are about the long-term average, over about 20 years, not a single hot year. The bars show how close today's long-term warming is to each limit.

How we calculate this

Copernicus measures the temperature of the air near the ground everywhere on Earth, every month, and compares it with the 1991-2020 average. That average was already 0.88°C warmer than before industry (1850 to 1900), so we add 0.88°C to show warming since pre-industrial times, as Copernicus does. Only 12-month and yearly averages are shown this way, because single months use slightly different offsets.

recent   = average of the last 12 months + 0.88 °C
long-term = straight line through the last 30 years, read today
countdown = (1.5 °C - long-term) ÷ warming per year

The long-term level smooths out natural ups and downs, such as El Niño years, the way the Paris limits are meant. The countdown assumes warming keeps today's pace; it would slow if the world cut emissions fast. Countries are compared with their own 1991-2020 normal, for the latest complete year.

Why a degree and a half matters

🤒 Think of a fever. Your body is healthy at about 37°C. Just 1.5°C more and you feel ill; 2°C more and you need a doctor. The planet is similar: a small rise in the average means many more heatwaves, droughts, floods and fires.

What causes it

Burning coal, oil and gas releases carbon dioxide and other gases that trap heat, like a blanket around the Earth. Warming stops only when the world's CO₂ emissions reach net zero. The Emissions Clock shows them live.
